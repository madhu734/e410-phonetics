TOPIC 1

Phonetics and Phonology: Definitions, Differences, Classification & Correlation

1.1 What is Phonetics?

Definition

Phonetics is the scientific study of how humans physically produce, transmit, and hear speech sounds. It does not depend on any specific language — it is universal.

Think of phonetics as the "hardware" of speech. It looks at what your mouth, tongue, lips, and vocal cords actually do when you make sounds.

Three Branches of Phonetics

  1. Articulatory Phonetics — How we physically produce sounds using our mouth, tongue, lips, etc. This is the most important branch for ELT teachers because we need to show students where to put their tongue.
  2. Acoustic Phonetics — How sound waves travel through the air (frequency, pitch, loudness). Studied with spectrograms in labs.
  3. Auditory Phonetics — How our ears and brain receive and decode speech sounds.

1.2 What is Phonology?

Definition

Phonology is the study of how sounds work within a specific language's mental system. It looks at which sounds can change meaning and what rules govern their combination.

Think of phonology as the "software" — the mental rules in your brain. For example, English treats /p/ and /b/ as different sounds because swapping them changes meaning (pin vs bin). But in Arabic, [p] is just a variant of /b/.

1.4 How are Phonetics and Phonology Related?

They need each other. Phonetics gives the raw physical data; Phonology gives the mental rules that organize those sounds into a meaningful system. Without Phonetics, Phonology has no physical basis. Without Phonology, Phonetics is just meaningless noise data.

1.5 Graphemes vs Phonemes: Why English Spelling is Tricky

A grapheme is a written letter (<c>, <ph>). A phoneme is a spoken sound (/k/, /f/). In English, there is no one-to-one match between letters and sounds:

  • The letter <c> can be /k/ (cut), /s/ (nice), or /ʃ/ (ocean)
  • The sound /iː/ has 7+ spellings: he, believe, Caesar, see, people, seize, seas
  • The -ough spelling has 7 different sounds: tough /ʌf/, cough /ɒf/, bough /aʊ/, though /əʊ/, through /uː/, borough /ə/, hiccough /ʌp/

George Bernard Shaw's joke: "GHOTI" = "FISH" (gh from tough = /f/, o from women = /ɪ/, ti from nation = /ʃ/).

Homophones vs Homographs

  • Homophones: Same sound, different spelling — sea /siː/ vs see /siː/
  • Homographs: Same spelling, different sound — lead /liːd/ (guide) vs lead /led/ (metal)

1.6 The IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet)

Founded in 1886 by Paul Passy. Golden Rule: "One symbol for one sound, and one sound for one symbol."

Why ELT Teachers Need the IPA

  1. Look-up: Find correct pronunciation in dictionaries
  2. Diagnosis: Identify exactly which sound a student gets wrong
  3. Explanation: Teach stress, weak forms, and connected speech clearly
  4. Materials: Create pronunciation drills and worksheets

1.7 Organs of Speech

Humans don't have special "speech organs." We use breathing and eating organs to produce speech:

  • Lungs — Air source (all English sounds use pulmonic egressive airstream)
  • Larynx & Vocal Cords — Voicing (vibrating = voiced, open = voiceless)
  • Tongue — Most flexible organ (Tip, Blade, Front, Back, Root)
  • Lips — Spread, neutral, or rounded
  • Palate — Hard palate (fixed) and Soft palate/Velum (moves up/down)
  • Alveolar Ridge — The bumpy ridge behind upper front teeth
  • Teeth — Upper and lower front teeth

Active articulators (move): tongue, lower lip. Passive articulators (stay still): upper teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate.

Velum up = oral sound (most sounds). Velum down = nasal sound (/m, n, ŋ/).

1.9 Classification of English Vowels (20 sounds)

Vowels are produced with no blockage of airflow. All vowels are voiced. They are described by 4 features:

  1. Tongue Height: Close (high), Mid, Open (low)
  2. Tongue Position: Front, Central, Back
  3. Lip Shape: Spread, Neutral, Rounded
  4. Length: Long (tense) or Short (lax)

The Schwa /ə/ — The King of English Sounds

The schwa is the most frequent vowel in English. It appears in every unstressed syllable. Your mouth is completely relaxed — neutral lips, neutral tongue. Words like to, of, a, the, and all use schwa in normal speech.

TOPIC 2

Phonemes, Phones & Allophones: Definitions, Differences & Theories

2.1 Three Core Concepts

Phone — Notation: [ ]

Any speech sound as a physical, acoustic event. It can be measured with instruments. Example: aspirated [pʰ] and unaspirated [p] are two different phones.

Phoneme — Notation: / /

The smallest abstract sound unit in a language that can change meaning. It lives in the speaker's mind. Example: /p/ vs /b/ — swapping them changes pat to bat. So /p/ and /b/ are separate phonemes.

Allophone — Notation: [ ]

A predictable variant of a phoneme that depends on context. It never changes meaning. Example: [pʰ] (in pin) and [p] (in spin) are allophones of the phoneme /p/.

2.2 Key Tests

Minimal Pair Test: Two words that differ by only one sound in the same position and have different meanings (e.g., cat vs bat) → the differing sounds are separate phonemes.

Commutation Test (Trubetzkoy): Swap one sound for another. If meaning changes → phonemes. If only pronunciation style changes → allophones.

Complementary Distribution: Two allophones never appear in the same environment. [pʰ] only appears syllable-initially, [p] only appears after /s/. They are in complementary distribution.

2.3 Five Major Theories (Covered in Class)

1. Daniel Jones (1881–1967) — Foundations of Phonetics

British phonetician at UCL. Helped build the IPA. Created the Cardinal Vowels system — 8 universal reference points for describing vowels based on extreme tongue positions. These don't belong to any specific language.

2. Nikolai Trubetzkoy (1890–1938) — Birth of Phonology

Founder of the Prague Linguistic Circle (1926). Published Grundzüge der Phonologie (Principles of Phonology, 1939). Key contributions:

  • Separated Phonetics (physical sounds) from Phonology (mental sound system)
  • Neutralization: When two phonemes lose their contrast in a specific position (e.g., German Rad /d/ and Rat /t/ both become [t] at the end of a word)
  • Archiphoneme: Written with a capital letter (e.g., /T/) to represent the neutralized pair

3. Roman Jakobson (1896–1982) — Distinctive Features

Proved that sounds are not invisible atoms but bundles of binary features. Each feature is either present [+] or absent [-]:

  • [±voiced]: /b/ [+voiced] vs /p/ [-voiced]
  • [±nasal]: /m/ [+nasal] vs /b/ [-nasal]
  • [±continuant]: /f/ [+continuant] vs /p/ [-continuant]

4. Noam Chomsky (1928–) — Generative Phonology

Saw language as a mental system. Phonology = mental grammar rules that derive what we actually say:

Underlying Form (UR) → [Phonological Rules] → Surface Form (SR)

5. Morris Halle (1923–2018) — SPE (1968)

Co-authored The Sound Pattern of English with Chomsky (1968). Added an extensive articulatory feature system ([±coronal], [±anterior], etc.) and established strictly ordered rules.

2.4 Phonological Rule Notation

A → B / C __ D

"Sound A becomes B when it occurs between C and D"

Four Main Sound Change Processes

  1. Assimilation: A sound becomes more like its neighbor. /ɪn + pʊt/ → [ɪmpʊt]
  2. Dissimilation: Two similar sounds become different. Latin peregrinus → English pilgrim
  3. Deletion/Elision: A sound disappears. postman /ˈpəʊstmən/ → [ˈpəʊsmən]
  4. Insertion/Epenthesis: An extra sound is added. warmth → [wɔːmpθ]

TOPIC 3

Syllable: Phonetic vs. Phonological Definitions & Phonotactic Possibilities

3.1 What is a Syllable?

A syllable is a single beat or pulse in speech. It has one vowel sound (or vowel-like sound) at its center, with optional consonants around it.

  • Cat = 1 syllable [kæt]
  • Paper = 2 syllables [peɪ.pər]
  • Banana = 3 syllables [bə.næn.ə]

How to count syllables: Place your hand under your chin. Each time your jaw drops for a vowel sound = 1 syllable.

3.2 Phonetic vs Phonological Syllable

FeaturePhonetic SyllablePhonological Syllable
NaturePhysical, acoustic (peaks of loudness)Abstract, mental (grammar rules)
Defined bySonority peaks and chest pulsesStructure: Onset + Nucleus + Coda
Language-specific?Mostly universalStrictly language-specific rules
BoundariesFuzzy/continuousClear and categorical

Sonority

Sonority = how loud/resonant a sound is. The Sonority Hierarchy from most to least: Vowels > Glides (/j, w/) > Liquids (/r, l/) > Nasals (/m, n, ŋ/) > Fricatives > Stops.

Sonority Sequencing Principle (SSP): In a well-formed syllable, sonority rises from the onset to the nucleus and falls toward the coda.

3.3 Internal Structure of a Syllable

σ = Onset (optional consonants) + Rhyme [Nucleus (obligatory vowel) + Coda (optional consonants)]
  • Onset: Consonant(s) before the vowel. Example: /tr/ in train
  • Nucleus: The vowel — the core of every syllable. Can also be a syllabic consonant (/l̩/ in bottle)
  • Coda: Consonant(s) after the vowel. Example: /ts/ in cats

3.4 Open vs Closed Syllables

  • Open: Ends with a vowel (no coda) — go, me, pa-per. Vowel tends to be long.
  • Closed: Ends with consonant(s) (has coda) — cat, dog, help. Vowel tends to be short.

3.5 Phonotactics

Phonotactics = the rules about which sound combinations are allowed in a language.

English Syllable Template: (C)(C)(C) V (C)(C)(C)(C) — up to 3 consonants in onset, up to 4 in coda.

3-Consonant Onset Rule

If an English syllable starts with 3 consonants: C₁ must be /s/, C₂ must be a voiceless stop (/p, t, k/), C₃ must be an approximant (/l, r, w, j/).

Examples: spring /spr-/, street /str-/, split /spl-/, scream /skr-/, squeak /skw-/

Maximum Coda (up to 4 consonants)

Examples: texts /teksts/, sixths /sɪksθs/, glimpsed /ɡlɪmpst/

SSP Violation: /s/ + Stop

In stop /stɒp/, sonority drops from /s/ to /t/ before rising to the vowel. This breaks the SSP. Solution: /s/ is treated as an "appendix" outside the core syllable.

Maximal Onset Principle (MOP)

When splitting multisyllabic words, assign as many consonants as possible to the onset of the next syllable (if the resulting cluster is legal). Example: diploma → /dɪ.ˈpləʊ.mə/ (because /pl/ is a valid onset).

TOPIC 4

Connected Speech and Rules

4.1 What is Connected Speech?

Definition

Connected speech is natural, continuous spoken language where words blend together and sounds get modified. It is the opposite of saying each word separately (citation form).

Why does it happen? Not laziness — it is natural speech efficiency:

  1. Principle of Least Effort: Our mouth saves energy by taking shortcuts
  2. Coarticulation: The tongue prepares for the next sound before finishing the current one
  3. Stress-Timing: English squeezes unstressed syllables to keep rhythm even

4.2 Linking (Catenation)

When the final consonant of one word joins smoothly to the initial vowel of the next word:

  • "Turn it on" → [tɜː.nɪ.tɒn] (sounds like "tur-ni-ton")
  • "Pick it up" → [pɪ.kɪ.tʌp] (sounds like "pi-ki-tup")
  • "An apple" → [ə.næpl]

Vowel-to-Vowel Linking (Glide Insertion)

  • Linking /r/: When spelling has an 'r' that's normally silent (in RP), it's pronounced before a vowel: "far away" → /fɑːr əˈweɪ/
  • Linking /w/: After rounded vowels (/uː, əʊ, aʊ/): "go on" → /ɡəʊ w ɒn/
  • Linking /j/: After front vowels (/iː, eɪ, aɪ/): "I am" → /aɪ j æm/

4.3 Intrusion

Like linking, but the inserted sound has no letter in the spelling:

  • Intrusive /r/: "law and order" → /lɔː r ənd ɔːdə/ (no 'r' in "law"!)
  • Intrusive /w/: "do it" → /duː w ɪt/
  • Intrusive /j/: "see it" → /siː j ɪt/

4.4 Assimilation

Definition

A sound changes to become more like its neighbor in Place, Manner, or Voicing.

Three Directions

  1. Regressive (Anticipatory): The following sound changes the preceding one (most common in English). "ten boys" → [tem bɔɪz]
  2. Progressive: The preceding sound changes the following one. Plural -s: "cats" /kæts/ (voiceless after /t/), "dogs" /dɒɡz/ (voiced after /ɡ/)
  3. Coalescent: Two sounds merge into a completely new sound. /t/ + /j/ → /tʃ/: "don't you" → /ˈdəʊntʃu/

4.5 Elision (Sound Deletion)

Definition

A sound that exists in careful speech is completely dropped in fast, natural speech.

/t/ and /d/ Deletion Rule

When /t/ or /d/ is between two other consonants (C + /t,d/ + C), it disappears:

  • "next week" → [neks wiːk]
  • "friendship" → [ˈfrenʃɪp]
  • "I can't swim" → [aɪ kɑːn swɪm]

Exception: If the next word starts with a vowel, /t/ and /d/ stay.

Schwa/Vowel Deletion (Syncope)

  • "camera" → /ˈkæmrə/ (3 → 2 syllables)
  • "history" → /ˈhɪstri/
  • "interesting" → /ˈɪntrəstɪŋ/
  • "chocolate" → /ˈtʃɒklət/

/h/ Dropping in Weak Forms

Unstressed pronouns/auxiliaries lose their /h/: "tell him" → /ˈtel ɪm/, "ask her" → /ˈɑːsk ɜː/

4.6 Glottalization

Replacing /t/ with a glottal stop [ʔ] — a brief closure of the vocal cords. Common in Cockney and Estuary English:

  • "bottle" → [ˈbɒʔl], "little" → [ˈlɪʔl], "football" → [ˈfʊʔbɔːl]

Three Theories of Glottalization

  1. Autosegmental Phonology: The glottal feature [constricted glottis] spreads to the /t/ slot while the tongue-tip closure is delinked
  2. Articulatory Phonology: The tongue-tip closure gesture is reduced/omitted; the glottal closure gesture takes over
  3. Usage-Based Theory: High-frequency words like bottle store [ʔ] variants directly in the mental lexicon

4.7 Flapping (American English)

In General American, /t/ and /d/ between vowels (before an unstressed syllable) become a quick tap [ɾ]:

  • "butter" → [ˈbʌɾər], "water" → [ˈwɑːɾər], "city" → [ˈsɪɾi]

4.8 Juncture

The acoustic clues that tell listeners where word boundaries are. Classic example:

"I scream" /aɪ + skriːm/"Ice cream" /ˈaɪs + kriːm/
Full /aɪ/ diphthong; /k/ is unaspirated (after /s/)Short /aɪ/ (pre-fortis clipping); /k/ is aspirated [kʰ] (word-initial)

4.9 Stress-Timing & Rhythm

English is a stress-timed language: stressed syllables come at roughly equal intervals, regardless of how many unstressed syllables are in between. Unstressed syllables get squeezed. Bangla is syllable-timed: every syllable gets equal time.

4.10 Intonation

  • Rising ↗: Yes/No questions, politeness, uncertainty — "Are you coming? ↗"
  • Falling ↘: Statements, Wh-questions, finality — "I am ready. ↘"

4.11 Contractions & Reductions

  • Standard contractions: I'm, you'll, she's, they'll
  • Fast speech reductions: "going to" → gonna, "want to" → wanna, "got to" → gotta, "what are you" → whatcha
TOPIC 5

Strong vs Weak Form Words: Contractions, Elision & Assimilation

5.1 What are Weak Forms?

Definition

Weak forms are the reduced, unstressed pronunciations of function words (articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliaries, conjunctions). Vowels get reduced to schwa /ə/ or dropped entirely.

Why? English is stress-timed. Content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) get stress. Function words get squeezed into weak forms to keep the rhythm.

5.3 When Must You Use the Strong Form?

  1. End of a sentence: "What are you looking for?" → for = /fɔː/
  2. Contrastive stress: "I said AND, not OR!" → and = /ænd/
  3. Coordinated prepositions: "to and from London" → /tuː/, /frɒm/
  4. Quoting the word itself: "The word 'that' has four letters." → /ðæt/

5.4 Connection to Other Topics

Weak forms involve elision (dropping /h/ from him, her, have), assimilation (sounds changing to match neighbors), and vowel reduction (full vowels → schwa). These are all connected speech processes that work together.

TOPIC 6

IPA Transcription of Connected Speech

6.1 Putting It All Together

When transcribing connected speech, you must apply all the rules from Topics 1–5 simultaneously: weak forms, linking, assimilation, elision, and stress marking.

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Identify content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) — these keep their full (strong) pronunciation
  2. Identify function words (to, and, of, the, can, was, etc.) — reduce them to weak forms
  3. Mark primary stress (ˈ) on the main stressed syllable of content words
  4. Apply linking — connect final consonants to following initial vowels
  5. Apply assimilation — change alveolars before bilabials/velars
  6. Apply elision — drop /t/, /d/ in consonant clusters; drop /h/ in weak forms
  7. Apply contractions if appropriate

VIVA Q&A

Combined High-Yield Viva Question Bank

Below are key questions covering all 6 topics. Model answers are written in simple, clear English suitable for viva delivery.

Phonetics & Phonology

Q1. What is the difference between Phonetics and Phonology?
Phonetics studies the physical production and acoustic properties of speech sounds across all languages. Phonology studies how sounds function and form meaningful contrasts within a specific language's mental grammar.
Q2. What are the three branches of Phonetics? Which matters most for ELT?
Articulatory, Acoustic, and Auditory. Articulatory phonetics is most relevant to ELT because teachers need to show students where to place their tongue, lips, and teeth.
Q3. How are Phonetics and Phonology related?
They are complementary. Phonetics gives the physical raw material; Phonology gives the organizational rules. Neither is complete without the other.
Q4. What is the IPA and its golden rule?
The International Phonetic Alphabet, founded in 1886 by Paul Passy. Its golden rule: "One symbol for one sound, and one sound for one symbol."
Q5. What is the Schwa? Why is it the most common English sound?
The schwa /ə/ is a short, mid-central, neutral vowel made with a completely relaxed mouth. It's the most common because English is stress-timed, so all unstressed vowels tend to reduce to schwa.
Q6. Name the 3 parameters for describing consonants.
1. Voicing (voiced or voiceless), 2. Place of articulation (where the blockage is), 3. Manner of articulation (how air is released).
Q7. What is the difference between a Plosive and an Affricate?
Both start with complete blockage. In a plosive, the blockage is released suddenly with a burst. In an affricate, it is released slowly, creating friction.

Phonemes & Phones

Q8. What is the difference between a Phone and a Phoneme?
A phone is any physical speech sound, written in square brackets [ ]. A phoneme is an abstract mental sound unit that can change meaning, written in slashes / /.
Q9. What is an Allophone? Give an example.
An allophone is a predictable variant of a phoneme that never changes meaning. Example: aspirated [pʰ] in "pin" and unaspirated [p] in "spin" are both allophones of /p/.
Q10. What is Trubetzkoy's Commutation Test?
Swap one sound for another in a word. If meaning changes, the sounds are separate phonemes. If meaning stays the same, they are allophones.
Q11. What is Neutralization? Give an example.
Neutralization is when two phonemes lose their contrast in a specific position. In German, /d/ and /t/ both become [t] at the end of a word — "Rad" and "Rat" sound identical.
Q12. What is Jakobson's Distinctive Feature Theory?
Sounds are bundles of binary features, each marked as present [+] or absent [-]. For example, /b/ is [+voiced] and /p/ is [-voiced].
Q13. What is the SPE formula?
Underlying Representation → [Phonological Rules] → Surface Representation. Published by Chomsky and Halle in 1968.

Syllable & Phonotactics

Q14. How do phonetic and phonological syllables differ?
A phonetic syllable is a physical peak of sonority. A phonological syllable is an abstract mental unit with structural positions: onset, nucleus, and coda.
Q15. What is the English syllable template?
(C)(C)(C) V (C)(C)(C)(C) — up to 3 consonants in the onset and up to 4 in the coda. Example: "spring" (CCCVC), "texts" (CVCCCC).
Q16. What are the rules for a 3-consonant onset?
C₁ must be /s/, C₂ must be a voiceless stop (/p, t, k/), C₃ must be an approximant (/l, r, w, j/). Example: "spring" /spr-/, "street" /str-/.
Q17. Why do Bangla speakers say "is-kul" for "school"?
Bangla phonotactics doesn't allow initial /sk-/ clusters. Speakers apply prothesis — inserting a vowel /i/ before the cluster — to create two legal syllables: is + kul.

Connected Speech

Q18. What is connected speech? Is it lazy speech?
Connected speech is continuous, natural spoken language where words blend and sounds change. It is NOT laziness — it is governed by the Principle of Least Effort and natural coarticulation.
Q19. What is the difference between Linking /r/ and Intrusive /r/?
Linking /r/ has an 'r' in the spelling that becomes audible before a vowel ("far away"). Intrusive /r/ has NO 'r' in the spelling — it is inserted purely as a glide ("law and order").
Q20. Explain Coalescent Assimilation with examples.
Two adjacent sounds merge into a new third sound. /t/ + /j/ → /tʃ/ ("don't you" → /ˈdəʊntʃu/). /d/ + /j/ → /dʒ/ ("did you" → /ˈdɪdʒu/).
Q21. What is the rule for /t, d/ elision?
When /t/ or /d/ sits between two other consonants (C + /t,d/ + C), it is deleted: "next week" → [neks wiːk]. But if a vowel follows, the /t,d/ stays.
Q22. How do "I scream" and "Ice cream" differ acoustically?
In "I scream," the /aɪ/ is long and /k/ is unaspirated (after /s/). In "Ice cream," /aɪ/ is shortened (pre-fortis clipping) and /k/ is aspirated [kʰ] (word-initial).
Q23. What is the difference between Stress-Timed and Syllable-Timed languages?
In stress-timed languages (English), stressed syllables occur at equal intervals — unstressed syllables get compressed. In syllable-timed languages (Bangla), every syllable gets roughly equal time.

Strong vs Weak Forms

Q24. What are Weak Forms?
Reduced, unstressed pronunciations of function words where vowels become schwa /ə/ or get dropped. Example: "and" /ænd/ → /ən/.
Q25. When must you use the Strong Form?
1. At the end of a sentence. 2. For emphasis or contrast. 3. In coordinated prepositions. 4. When quoting the word itself.

IPA Transcription

Q26. How do you transcribe connected speech step by step?
1. Identify content words (full pronunciation). 2. Reduce function words to weak forms. 3. Mark stress. 4. Apply linking. 5. Apply assimilation. 6. Apply elision. 7. Apply contractions.
Q27. Transcribe "He should have told them."
/hi ˈʃʊd əv ˈtəʊld əm/ — "he" keeps /h/ (sentence-initial), "should" → weak /ʃʊd/, "have" → /əv/ (h-drop), "them" → /əm/ (weak form).
Q28. What is Pre-fortis Clipping?
A vowel is shortened when followed by a voiceless consonant in the same syllable. Compare: "bead" [biːd] (long) vs "beat" [biˑt] (shortened before voiceless /t/).
Q29. What is a Syllabic Consonant?
A consonant that acts as the core (nucleus) of a syllable instead of a vowel. English /n/ and /l/ often do this: "button" /ˈbʌtn̩/, "little" /ˈlɪtl̩/.
Q30. What is a Minimal Pair and why is it useful in ELT?
Two words differing by one sound in the same position with different meanings (e.g., "ship" vs "sheep"). In ELT, minimal pairs help students identify and practice difficult sound contrasts.
