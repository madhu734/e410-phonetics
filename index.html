1 Phonetics & Phonology

Phonetics

The scientific study of how humans physically produce, transmit, and hear speech sounds. It is universal — it applies to all languages.

Phonology

The study of how sounds function and form meaningful patterns within a specific language's mental system.

Three Branches of Phonetics

  • Articulatory — How we make sounds with our mouth/tongue/lips (most important for ELT teachers)
  • Acoustic — How sound waves travel through air (studied with spectrograms)
  • Auditory — How our ears and brain decode sounds

Key Differences

FeaturePhoneticsPhonology
NaturePhysical, concreteAbstract, mental
Core Question"How is this sound made?""How does it function in the language?"
ScopeUniversalLanguage-specific
UnitPhone [ ]Phoneme / /

Correlation

Phonetics gives the raw material; Phonology gives the organizing rules. Neither works without the other. Phonetics without Phonology = meaningless noise data. Phonology without Phonetics = empty abstract formalism.

1b Graphemes vs Phonemes

Grapheme = written letter (<c>). Phoneme = spoken sound (/k/). English has no one-to-one match:

  • <c> → /k/ in cut, /s/ in nice, /ʃ/ in ocean
  • /iː/ has 7+ spellings: he, believe, Caesar, see, people, seize, seas
  • -ough has 7 pronunciations: tough /ʌf/, through /uː/, though /əʊ/, etc.
Shaw's Joke

"GHOTI" = "FISH" — gh from tough = /f/, o from women = /ɪ/, ti from nation = /ʃ/

Homophones: Same sound, different spelling — sea vs see. Homographs: Same spelling, different sound — lead /liːd/ vs lead /led/.

1d Organs of Speech

All English sounds use pulmonic egressive airstream (air pushed out from lungs).

  • Lungs — air source
  • Larynx + Vocal Cords — vibrating = voiced; open = voiceless
  • Tongue (Tip, Blade, Front, Back, Root) — most flexible organ
  • Velum — raised = oral sounds; lowered = nasal sounds (/m, n, ŋ/)
  • Lips, Teeth, Alveolar Ridge, Hard Palate

Active articulators (move): tongue, lower lip. Passive (stay still): upper teeth, ridge, hard palate.

2 Phonemes, Phones & Allophones

Phone [ ]

Any physical speech sound. Measurable with instruments. Example: [pʰ] and [p] are different phones.

Phoneme / /

Smallest abstract sound unit that can change meaning. Example: /p/ vs /b/ — pat vs bat.

Allophone [ ]

A predictable variant of a phoneme. Never changes meaning. Example: [pʰ] in pin and [p] in spin.

How to Tell Phonemes from Allophones

  • Minimal Pair Test: Swap one sound → meaning changes = phonemes
  • Commutation Test (Trubetzkoy): Same thing, more formal
  • Complementary Distribution: Two sounds never appear in the same environment = allophones

2b 5 Major Theories

1. Daniel Jones — Foundations of Phonetics

British phonetician at UCL. Built the IPA. Created the Cardinal Vowels — 8 universal reference points for describing vowels.

2. Trubetzkoy (1890–1938) — Birth of Phonology

Prague School founder. Grundzüge der Phonologie (1939). Key ideas:

  • Neutralization: Two phonemes lose their contrast in specific positions
  • Archiphoneme: Capital letter symbol for neutralized pair (e.g., /T/)

3. Jakobson (1896–1982) — Distinctive Features

Sounds = bundles of binary features: [+voiced], [-voiced], [+nasal], etc.

4. Chomsky — Generative Phonology

Underlying Form (UR) → [Rules] → Surface Form (SR)

5. Halle — SPE (1968)

Co-authored with Chomsky. Extended articulatory feature system + strictly ordered rules.

2c Phonological Rules

A → B / C __ D
"A becomes B between C and D"

Four Processes

  1. Assimilation: Sound becomes like neighbor — /ɪn+pʊt/ → [ɪmpʊt]
  2. Dissimilation: Similar sounds become different
  3. Deletion: Sound disappears — postman → [ˈpəʊsmən]
  4. Insertion: Extra sound added — warmth → [wɔːmpθ]

3 Syllable

A syllable = one beat in speech, centered on a vowel. Count syllables by counting jaw drops.

3b Syllable Structure

σ = Onset (C) + Rhyme [Nucleus (V) + Coda (C)]
  • Open syllable: No coda — go, me (vowel tends to be long)
  • Closed syllable: Has coda — cat, dog (vowel tends to be short)
  • Syllabic consonants: /l̩/ in bottle, /n̩/ in button

3c Phonotactics

English template: (C)(C)(C) V (C)(C)(C)(C) — up to 3 onset, 4 coda consonants.

3-Consonant Onset Rule

C₁ = /s/, C₂ = voiceless stop (/p,t,k/), C₃ = approximant (/l,r,w,j/). Examples: spring, street, split

SSP Violation

In stop /stɒp/, sonority drops from /s/ to /t/ — violates the rule. /s/ is treated as an "appendix."

Maximal Onset Principle

Assign consonants to the next syllable's onset if the cluster is legal: diploma → /dɪ.ˈpləʊ.mə/

Bangla Loanword Adaptation

  • Prothesis: Insert vowel at start — school → is-kul
  • Anaptyxis: Insert vowel in middle — glass → ge-las

4 Connected Speech

Definition

Natural continuous speech where words blend and sounds change. NOT laziness — it's governed by economy of effort, coarticulation, and stress-timing.

4b Linking & Intrusion

Catenation (C→V Linking)

Final consonant joins the next word's initial vowel:

  • "Turn it on" → [tɜː.nɪ.tɒn]
  • "Pick it up" → [pɪ.kɪ.tʌp]

V→V Linking (Glide Insertion)

TypeAfterExample
Linking /r/Spelling has 'r'"far away" → /fɑːr əˈweɪ/
Linking /w/Rounded V (/uː, əʊ/)"go on" → /ɡəʊ w ɒn/
Linking /j/Front V (/iː, eɪ, aɪ/)"I am" → /aɪ j æm/

Intrusion

Same as linking, but NO letter in spelling:

  • Intrusive /r/: "law and order" → /lɔː r ənd ɔːdə/
  • Intrusive /w/: "do it" → /duː w ɪt/

4c Assimilation

A sound changes to become more like its neighbor.

Directions

  1. Regressive: Following sound changes the preceding one (most common) — "ten boys" → [tem bɔɪz]
  2. Progressive: Preceding changes the following — plural -s: /s/ after voiceless, /z/ after voiced
  3. Coalescent: Two sounds merge into new one — /t/+/j/ → /tʃ/

4d Elision, Glottalization & Flapping

Elision (/t,d/ Deletion)

When /t/ or /d/ sits between two consonants: "next week" → [neks wiːk], "friendship" → [ˈfrenʃɪp]

Schwa Deletion

"camera" → /ˈkæmrə/, "interesting" → /ˈɪntrəstɪŋ/

Glottalization

/t/ replaced by glottal stop [ʔ]: "bottle" → [ˈbɒʔl]. Three theories: Autosegmental (feature spreading), Articulatory (gesture reduction), Usage-Based (stored variants).

Flapping (American English)

/t,d/ between vowels → quick tap [ɾ]: "butter" → [ˈbʌɾər], "water" → [ˈwɑːɾər]

Other Features

  • Juncture: "I scream" vs "Ice cream" — aspiration and vowel length differ
  • Stress-Timing: English = stress-timed (equal intervals between stresses). Bangla = syllable-timed.
  • Intonation: Rising ↗ = questions/politeness. Falling ↘ = statements/finality.
  • Contractions: I'm, you'll, gonna, wanna, gotta, whatcha

5 Strong vs Weak Forms

Weak Forms

Reduced unstressed pronunciations of function words. Vowels reduce to schwa /ə/ or get dropped. This maintains English stress-timed rhythm.

When to Use Strong Form

  1. End of sentence: "What are you looking for?" → /fɔː/
  2. Emphasis: "I said AND, not OR!" → /ænd/
  3. Coordinated: "to and from" → /ænd/
  4. Quoting: "The word 'that'" → /ðæt/

6 IPA Transcription of Connected Speech

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Identify content words → keep full pronunciation
  2. Reduce function words → weak forms
  3. Mark stress ˈ
  4. Apply linking (C→V connections)
  5. Apply assimilation (place/voice changes)
  6. Apply elision (/t,d/ deletion, h-dropping)
  7. Apply contractions

Phonetics & Phonology

Q1. What is the difference between Phonetics and Phonology? (click to reveal)
Phonetics studies the physical production and acoustic properties of speech sounds across all languages. Phonology studies how sounds function and form meaningful contrasts within a specific language's mental grammar.
Q2. Which branch of Phonetics matters most for ELT?
Articulatory phonetics — because teachers need to show students where to place their tongue, lips, and teeth to produce English sounds correctly.
Q3. How are Phonetics and Phonology related?
They are complementary. Phonetics gives the physical raw material; Phonology gives the organizational rules. Neither works without the other.
Q4. What is the IPA and its golden rule?
The International Phonetic Alphabet, founded in 1886 by Paul Passy. Golden rule: "One symbol for one sound, and one sound for one symbol."
Q5. What is the Schwa? Why is it the most common English sound?
The schwa /ə/ is a short, mid-central, neutral vowel made with a completely relaxed mouth. It's the most common because English is stress-timed, so all unstressed vowels reduce to schwa.
Q6. Name the 3 parameters for describing consonants.
1. Voicing (voiced or voiceless), 2. Place of articulation (where the blockage is), 3. Manner of articulation (how air is released).

Phonemes & Phones

Q7. What is a Phone vs a Phoneme?
A phone is any physical speech sound [ ]. A phoneme is an abstract mental sound that can change meaning / /. Example: /p/ vs /b/ changes "pat" to "bat."
Q8. What is an Allophone?
A predictable variant of a phoneme that never changes meaning. Aspirated [pʰ] in "pin" and unaspirated [p] in "spin" are allophones of /p/.
Q9. What is Trubetzkoy's Commutation Test?
Swap one sound for another. If meaning changes → phonemes. If not → allophones. Named after Nikolai Trubetzkoy, Prague School (1939).
Q10. What is Jakobson's Distinctive Feature Theory?
Sounds are bundles of binary features marked [+] or [-]. Example: /b/ is [+voiced], /p/ is [-voiced].
Q11. What is the SPE formula?
UR → [Phonological Rules] → SR. Published by Chomsky and Halle in 1968 (The Sound Pattern of English).
Q12. What is Neutralization?
When two phonemes lose their contrast in a specific position. German example: both "Rad" /d/ and "Rat" /t/ become [t] at the end of a word.

Syllable & Phonotactics

Q13. How do phonetic and phonological syllables differ?
Phonetic = physical peak of sonority. Phonological = abstract mental unit with onset, nucleus, and coda.
Q14. What is the English syllable template?
(C)(C)(C) V (C)(C)(C)(C) — up to 3 onset, 4 coda. Examples: "spring" (CCCVC), "texts" (CVCCCC).
Q15. Why do Bangla speakers say "is-kul" for "school"?
Bangla doesn't allow initial /sk-/ clusters. Prothesis (vowel insertion at the start) creates two legal syllables: is + kul.
Q16. What is the Sonority Sequencing Principle?
Sonority must rise from onset to nucleus and fall toward coda. Exception: /s/+stop clusters violate this — /s/ is treated as an extrametrical appendix.

Connected Speech

Q17. What is connected speech? Is it lazy speech?
Natural continuous speech where sounds blend. NOT laziness — it's governed by the Principle of Least Effort and natural coarticulation.
Q18. Linking /r/ vs Intrusive /r/?
Linking /r/: spelling has 'r' ("far away"). Intrusive /r/: NO 'r' in spelling ("law and order").
Q19. Explain Coalescent Assimilation.
Two sounds merge into a new one. /t/+/j/ → /tʃ/ ("don't you" → /ˈdəʊntʃu/). /d/+/j/ → /dʒ/ ("did you" → /ˈdɪdʒu/).
Q20. What is the /t,d/ elision rule?
C + /t,d/ + C → middle /t,d/ is deleted. "next week" → [neks wiːk]. But if a vowel follows, /t,d/ stays.
Q21. "I scream" vs "Ice cream" — how do they differ?
"I scream": long /aɪ/, unaspirated /k/ (after /s/). "Ice cream": short /aɪ/ (pre-fortis clipping), aspirated [kʰ] (word-initial).
Q22. Stress-Timed vs Syllable-Timed?
Stress-timed (English): equal intervals between stresses, unstressed syllables compressed. Syllable-timed (Bangla): every syllable gets equal time.

Strong vs Weak Forms

Q23. What are Weak Forms?
Reduced unstressed pronunciations of function words. Vowels reduce to schwa /ə/. Example: "and" /ænd/ → /ən/.
Q24. When must you use the Strong Form?
1. End of sentence, 2. Emphasis/contrast, 3. Coordinated prepositions, 4. Quoting the word itself.

IPA Transcription

Q25. How do you transcribe connected speech?
1. Content words → full form. 2. Function words → weak forms. 3. Mark stress. 4. Apply linking. 5. Apply assimilation. 6. Apply elision. 7. Apply contractions.
Q26. Transcribe "He should have told them."
/hi ˈʃʊd əv ˈtəʊld əm/ — "have" → /əv/ (h-drop), "them" → /əm/ (weak form).
Q27. What is Pre-fortis Clipping?
A vowel is shortened before a voiceless consonant in the same syllable. "bead" [biːd] (long) vs "beat" [biˑt] (short).
Q28. What is a Syllabic Consonant?
A consonant acting as a syllable's nucleus instead of a vowel. English /n/ and /l/ do this: "button" /ˈbʌtn̩/, "little" /ˈlɪtl̩/.
Q29. What is a Minimal Pair?
Two words differing by one sound in the same position with different meanings. "ship" vs "sheep." Useful in ELT for practicing difficult sound contrasts.
Q30. What is Complementary Distribution?
When two allophones never appear in the same environment. [pʰ] only at syllable-initial, [p] only after /s/. They are in complementary distribution.
