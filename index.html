Section A: Phonetics and Phonology

▼

1. Definition of Phonetics

Phonetics is the branch of linguistics that studies the physical properties of speech sounds. It deals with how speech sounds are produced by the human vocal organs, how they travel through the air as sound waves, and how they are heard and processed by the listener's ear.

In simple words, phonetics looks at the actual sounds that humans make when they speak. It does not care whether a sound changes the meaning of a word or not — it simply describes and classifies all the sounds that the human voice can produce.

2. Definition of Phonology

Phonology is the branch of linguistics that studies how speech sounds function and form patterns within a particular language. While phonetics looks at sounds as physical events, phonology looks at sounds as part of a system — how they are organized, which sounds can be combined together, and which sound differences actually matter for meaning.

3. Differences Between Phonetics and Phonology

FeaturePhoneticsPhonology
FocusPhysical properties of soundsHow sounds function in a language
ApproachDescribes all possible human soundsAnalyzes sound patterns in a specific language
ScopeUniversal — applies to all languagesLanguage-specific

MeaningDoes not consider meaningFocuses on meaning-distinguishing sounds
NatureConcrete and physicalAbstract and mental

4. Classification of Phonetics

BranchStudiesStageKey Method
ArticulatoryHow sounds are producedSpeaker's productionVocal organ observation, MRI
AcousticHow sounds travel as wavesTransmissionSpectrogram analysis
AuditoryHow sounds are perceivedListener's perceptionHearing/perception tests

5. Relationship Between Phonetics and Phonology

  • Phonetics provides the raw material for phonology — you need to know what sounds exist before analyzing how they function.
  • Phonology organizes what phonetics describes — from thousands of possible sounds, phonology identifies which ones matter.
  • They share the same subject matter (speech sounds) but ask different questions.
  • A complete understanding of any language's sound system requires both.
💡 Example

In English, [p] and [pʰ] are phonetically different (one has a puff of air). But phonologically, they are the same phoneme /p/. Phonetics describes the difference; phonology explains it doesn't matter for meaning in English.

🎯 Remember for Viva
  • Phonetics = physical sounds (production, transmission, perception). Universal.
  • Phonology = sound patterns and functions. Language-specific.
  • Three branches: Articulatory, Acoustic, Auditory.
  • Phonetics uses [ ], Phonology uses / /.
  • They are interdependent: phonetics provides data, phonology organizes it.

Section B: Phonemes and Phones

▼

1. Phoneme vs. Phone

A phoneme is the smallest unit of sound that can distinguish meaning. It is abstract and written in / /. A phone is any actual speech sound produced — concrete and physical, written in [ ].

FeaturePhonemePhone
NatureAbstract, mental unitConcrete, physical sound
Notation/p/[p]
NumberLimited (~44 in English)Unlimited variations
MeaningChanging one changes word meaningChanging one may not

2. Key Concepts

Allophone: A variant pronunciation of a phoneme. Example: [pʰ] (aspirated, in "pin") and [p] (unaspirated, in "spin") are allophones of /p/.

Minimal Pair: Two words differing by one phoneme — "pat" /pæt/ vs. "bat" /bæt/. Proves /p/ and /b/ are separate phonemes.

Complementary Distribution: Two phones never appear in the same environment — strong evidence they are allophones of the same phoneme.

Free Variation: Two phones can occur in the same position without changing meaning, and the choice is not predictable.

3. Important Scholars

🏛️ Nikolai Trubetzkoy (1890–1938)
Prague School of Linguistics • Principles of Phonology (1939)
  • Separated phonetics from phonology as distinct disciplines.
  • Defined phonemes by their distinctive function — ability to distinguish words.
  • Developed phonological oppositions — phonemes defined by contrasts (e.g., voiced vs. voiceless).
  • Introduced neutralization — when an opposition is cancelled in certain positions (e.g., German final devoicing).
  • Contributed to markedness theory — one member of a pair is "default" (unmarked), the other is "marked."
🔬 Roman Jakobson (1896–1982)
Prague School → Harvard/MIT • Distinctive Feature Theory
  • Proposed Distinctive Feature Theory — each phoneme is a bundle of smaller features.
  • Features are binary: either present (+) or absent (−). E.g., /b/ = [+voiced, +bilabial, +stop].
  • Used acoustic features (based on how sounds sound, not how they're made).
  • Believed features are universal — same set used across all languages.
  • Studied child language acquisition — children learn phonemes in a universal order.
📖 Daniel Jones (1881–1967)
British Phonetics Tradition • University College London
  • Defined the phoneme as a family of related sounds (concrete/physical approach).
  • Created the Cardinal Vowel System — reference points for describing vowels across all languages.
  • Key role in developing the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA).
  • Published the English Pronouncing Dictionary (EPD) and described Received Pronunciation (RP).
🧠 Noam Chomsky & Morris Halle
MIT • The Sound Pattern of English (SPE, 1968)
  • Introduced Generative Phonology: speakers have an underlying representation of words, and phonological rules convert it to the surface pronunciation.
  • Formula: Underlying → Rules → Surface.
  • Expanded Jakobson's features using articulatory-based features ([±voice], [±nasal], [±continuant], etc.).
  • Argued the classical phoneme concept was unnecessary — replaced by underlying segments + rules.
🎯 Remember for Viva
  • Phoneme = abstract (/ /). Phone = concrete ([ ]). Allophone = variant of a phoneme.
  • Minimal pairs prove phoneme status. Complementary distribution proves allophone status.
  • Trubetzkoy: oppositions, neutralization. Jakobson: distinctive features (binary, acoustic).
  • Jones: concrete phoneme, Cardinal Vowels, IPA. Chomsky & Halle: SPE, generative phonology.

Section C: English Vowels

▼

A vowel is a sound made when air flows freely through the mouth without obstruction. Vowels are classified by: tongue height (close/mid/open), tongue position (front/central/back), and lip rounding (rounded/unrounded). English RP has 12 monophthongs + 8 diphthongs + 5 triphthongs = 25 vowel phonemes.

Section D: English Consonants

▼

Section E: Syllable

▼

A syllable is a unit of pronunciation with one sonority peak (usually a vowel). Phonetic definition: a peak of sonority surrounded by less sonorous sounds. Phonological definition: a structural unit with positions (onset, nucleus, coda).

Syllable Structure

🏗️ Structure

Onset (consonants before vowel) + Nucleus (vowel — required) + Coda (consonants after vowel)

Rhyme = Nucleus + Coda. Open syllable = no coda ("go"). Closed syllable = has coda ("cat").

Example: "cat" /kæt/ → Onset: /k/, Nucleus: /æ/, Coda: /t/

Phonotactic Rules

  • Onset max = 3 consonants: /s/ + voiceless plosive + approximant (e.g., "string" /strɪŋ/)
  • Coda max = 4 consonants: (e.g., "texts" /teksts/)
  • Some combinations forbidden: English words can't start with /ŋ/
PatternExample
V"a" /ə/
CV"go" /ɡəʊ/
CVC"cat" /kæt/
CCVC"stop" /stɒp/
CCCVC"string" /strɪŋ/
🎯 Remember for Viva
  • Syllable = one sonority peak. Structure: Onset + Nucleus + Coda.
  • Nucleus is required; onset and coda are optional.
  • English onset max 3C, coda max 4C. Sonority Sequencing Principle governs clusters.

Section F: Strong & Weak Forms, Contractions, Elision, Assimilation

▼

Strong and Weak Forms

Many function words have two pronunciations: a strong form (full, stressed) and a weak form (reduced, unstressed). Weak forms are the normal way of speaking.

WordStrongWeakExample
the/ðiː//ðə/"Give me the book" /ðə bʊk/
to/tuː//tə/"I want to go" /tə ɡəʊ/
and/ænd//ən/"bread and butter" /ən/
can/kæn//kən/"I can see" /kən siː/
of/ɒv//əv/"cup of tea" /kʌpə tiː/
for/fɔː//fə/"This is for you" /fə juː/
have/hæv//əv/"I have done it" /aɪv dʌn ɪt/
are/ɑː//ə/"We are going" /wi ə ɡəʊɪŋ/

Elision

Elision = dropping a sound in connected speech. Most common: /t/ and /d/ in consonant clusters.

  • "next day" → /neks deɪ/ (/t/ dropped)
  • "hand bag" → /hæn bæɡ/ (/d/ dropped)
  • "family" → /fæmli/ (vowel dropped)
  • "Tell him" → /tel ɪm/ (/h/ dropped)

Assimilation

Assimilation = a sound changes to match a neighbour.

TypeDirectionExample
RegressiveChanges because of following sound"ten boys" → /tem bɔɪz/ (n→m before bilabial)
ProgressiveChanges because of preceding sound"cats" /kæts/ (-s voiceless after /t/)
CoalescentTwo sounds merge into one"don't you" → /dəʊntʃuː/ (t+j→tʃ)

Elision vs. Assimilation

FeatureElisionAssimilation
What happensSound is deletedSound is changed
ResultFewer soundsSame number, different sound
Example"last night" /lɑːs naɪt/"ten boys" /tem bɔɪz/
🎯 Remember for Viva
  • Weak forms = normal speech. Strong forms = emphasis/sentence-final/contrast.
  • Elision = deletion. Assimilation = change. Know the difference!
  • Three assimilation types: regressive (most common), progressive, coalescent.

Section G: Stress

▼

Stress = making a syllable more prominent. Four factors: loudness, length, pitch change (most important), vowel quality.

Levels of Stress

LevelSymbolExample
Primaryˈ/ˈwɔːtə/ "water"
Secondaryˌ/ˌiːvæljuˈeɪʃn̩/ "evaluation"
Unstressed(none)The "-ter" in "water"

Word Stress Rules

PatternRuleExamples
2-syllable nounsStress on 1st syllableˈwater, ˈtable, ˈmoney
2-syllable verbsStress on 2nd syllablebeˈgin, aˈllow, deˈcide
Noun/verb pairsNoun=1st, Verb=2ndˈrecord / reˈcord, ˈpresent / preˈsent
-tion, -ic, -ityStress on preceding syllableeduˈcation, eˈlectric, uˈniversity
-ee, -eer, -eseStress on suffixemployˈee, volunˈteer, Japaˈnese
Compound nounsStress on 1st elementˈblackbird, ˈfootball

Sentence Stress

Content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) are stressed. Function words (articles, prepositions, pronouns) are unstressed. English is stress-timed — stressed syllables come at regular intervals.

Contrastive stress: "I didn't say HE stole it." "I didn't say he STOLE it."

Stress shift: thirˈteen → ˈthirteen ˈpeople (avoids stress clash).

🎯 Remember for Viva
  • 4 factors: loudness, length, pitch change (most important), vowel quality.
  • Nouns = 1st syllable. Verbs = 2nd syllable (general rule).
  • Content words stressed, function words unstressed in sentences.
  • English is stress-timed. Contrastive stress overrides normal patterns.

Section H: Chunking and Tone

▼

Chunking

Chunking = dividing speech into tone units (meaningful groups). Chunks follow grammatical and sense boundaries, separated by brief pauses.

💡 Example

"My brother | who lives in London | is a doctor." — Three tone units.

Types of Tone

ToneMovementFunctionsExample
Falling ↘Pitch goes downStatements, wh-questions, commands, finality"She's a ↘teacher."
Rising ↗Pitch goes upYes/no questions, uncertainty, politeness"Are you ↗coming?"
Fall-rise ↘↗Falls then risesReservation, "but...", partial agreement"It's ↘↗possible."
Rise-fall ↗↘Rises then fallsStrong feelings, surprise, sarcasm"That's ↗↘wonderful!"
Level →Stays flatBoredom, routine listing"Monday → Tuesday →..."

Tone Unit Structure

PartDescriptionRequired?
Pre-headUnstressed syllables before first stressOptional
HeadFirst stressed syllable → before tonicOptional
Tonic syllableMost prominent; carries the toneRequired
TailSyllables after the tonicOptional
🎯 Remember for Viva
  • Chunking = dividing into tone units at grammatical/sense boundaries.
  • 5 tones: falling, rising, fall-rise, rise-fall, level.
  • Tone unit: (Pre-head) + (Head) + Tonic syllable + (Tail). Only tonic is required.

Section I: Intonation

▼

Intonation = the pattern of pitch changes across a whole utterance — the "melody" of speech. It's not what you say, but how you say it.

Functions of Intonation

FunctionWhat it DoesExample
GrammaticalDistinguishes statements/questions"She's coming." ↘ vs. "She's coming?" ↗
AttitudinalExpresses attitude/emotion"Thank you" ↘ (grateful) vs. → (bored)
DiscourseManages conversation flowFalling = finished speaking. Rising = your turn.
AccentualHighlights focus/new info"I bought a ↘CAR" (focus on car)

Tag Questions and Intonation

TagIntonationMeaning
"It's cold, isn't it?"↘ FallingI expect you to agree.
"It's cold, isn't it?"↗ RisingGenuine question — I'm not sure.
🎯 Remember for Viva
  • Intonation = pitch pattern across an utterance (melody of speech).
  • 4 functions: grammatical, attitudinal, discourse, accentual.
  • Same sentence changes meaning with different intonation.
  • Falling tag = expecting agreement. Rising tag = genuine question.

Section J: IPA Transcription of Connected Speech

▼

Connected speech = natural flowing speech where words are not pronounced in isolation. Key processes: weak forms, elision, assimilation, linking, contractions.

Types of Linking

TypeWhenExample
Linking /r/Word-final "r" + vowel"far away" → /fɑːrəweɪ/
Intrusive /r/No "r" in spelling + vowel"law and order" → /lɔːrənd ɔːdə/
Linking /j/Front vowel + vowel"he asked" → /hiːjɑːskt/
Linking /w/Back rounded vowel + vowel"do it" → /duːwɪt/

Transcription Examples — Click Words to Hear

📝 Example 1: "I want to go to the shop."

Careful: /aɪ wɒnt tuː ɡəʊ tuː ðiː ʃɒp/

Connected: /aɪ wɒnə ɡəʊ tə ðə ʃɒp/

Changes: "want to" → /wɒnə/ (elision of /t/, weak "to"). "the" → /ðə/ (weak form).

📝 Example 2: "Can you give me a cup of tea?"

Careful: /kæn juː ɡɪv miː eɪ kʌp ɒv tiː/

Connected: /kən jə ɡɪv mi ə kʌpə tiː/

Changes: "can" → /kən/, "you" → /jə/, "a" → /ə/, "cup of" → /kʌpə/ (/v/ elided).

📝 Example 3: "What do you want for breakfast?"

Careful: /wɒt duː juː wɒnt fɔː brekfəst/

Connected: /wɒt dʒə wɒnt fə brekfəst/

Changes: "do you" → /dʒə/ (coalescent: d+j→dʒ), "for" → /fə/ (weak form).

📝 Example 4: "I told him that I would be there at ten."

Careful: /aɪ təʊld hɪm ðæt aɪ wʊd biː ðeə æt ten/

Connected: /aɪ təʊld ɪm ðət aɪd bi ðeər ət ten/

Changes: "him" → /ɪm/ (h-dropped), "that" → /ðət/, "I would" → /aɪd/ (contraction), "there at" → /ðeər ət/ (linking r), "at" → /ət/ (weak).

🎯 Remember for Viva
  • Connected speech = natural flowing speech with modifications.
  • Key processes: weak forms, elision, assimilation, linking, contractions.
  • 4 linking types: linking /r/, intrusive /r/, linking /j/, linking /w/.
  • Be able to transcribe sentences and identify which processes occurred.
